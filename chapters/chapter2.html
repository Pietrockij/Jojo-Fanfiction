Chapter 2 | D'Oliva Olio part 1

Stella, Naples, 1978

While his parents were yelling at each other, a young Olio turned up the volume on his radio once again; by now it had become a battle of volume, and the boy, despite the loud music, could still hear his parents' shouts, but he didn't feel sad—he was more annoyed. He needed to concentrate to continue his game of Risk: “Those two are always fighting!” “Those two are always fighting.”

In the other room, his parents kept hurling insults at each other. His father said, “If you want Olio to go to that private school, this is the only way,” while his mother retorted, “Oh, come on, but do you really want our child to grow up in Secondigliano!?” “I know, but do you really want our child to grow up in Secondigliano!?”

Frustration hung thick in the air, and with it, the tension. “We're both working two jobs each; we have to make a choice, either we keep the house, or we move and manage to send our son to that prestigious school,” the father said.

The mother listened intermittently, worried: “Our son will turn into a delinquent if we raise him in that dump…”

The father adopted a reassuring tone: “Olio is already grown up, and besides, he's a smart kid. I'm sure he can tell right from wrong. Plus, he'll spend most of his time at school, don't worry, "vita mj," our son will turn out just fine.”

The mother's anger gave way to sadness: “It's just so strange—he spends his days alone in his room playing Risiko. I'm worried about him!”

“I am too, but I'm sure that school will be able to make the most of his intelligence and passion.”

A few months later

Olio finally found himself standing before that monumental private school, alongside thousands of students from the upper classes, children of doctors, politicians, judges, and descendants of what remains of the pre-republican nobility.

The commotion of thousands of students fell silent at a single appearance: that of Principal Luigi Vuittone: “Welcome to San Gennaro High School. Here, the best of you will become even better; you will become the scum that forms our ruling class!”

At his side, a large gate was seized by two gatekeepers. “Let the gate open!” ordered the principal.

The gatekeepers, in unison with the bell, opened the gate, allowing the students to stream in.

“What the hell is wrong with this guy?” one student asked his friend.

“Who knows?”

Just then, Olio chimed in: “That's Principal Vuittone; they say he lost his mind in the war.”

“Okay,” the two friends replied, a bit bewildered, but they didn't have time to argue before Olio launched into an explanation: “When you play in Europe, you have to prepare your troops for anything, from the hot beaches of Greece to the frozen Russian steppe.”

The two students were dumbfounded: “What are you talking about?”

“I'm talking about Risk,” said Olio.

“It's just a stupid board game!”

Never had words been more offensive from his point of view, and, irritated, he replied with calculated coldness: “You don't want your own pieces to rebel, do you?”

“Man, this guy is really weird.”

“Gim, let's get out of here,” the boys said, disinterested and bewildered.

Later that same day…

After spending a boring day at school, Olio's parents came to pick him up along the busy roads that wind through the hills, connecting the southern part of town to the working-class neighborhoods.

Once they arrived home, Olio didn't even bother getting out of the car. Intrigued by the new neighborhood, he began exploring the area.

As he walked, he started to feel a gnawing hunger in his stomach, so after searching for hours among the high-rises, he found a café.

He sat down, but before he even had time to settle into his seat: “Hey, what are you doing! You can't just sit down without ordering anything!” the bartender snapped.

Olio didn't budge a muscle: “Then give me a coffee and a croissant.”

“Do you think I'm a waiter? Get your butt up and go get it yourself!”

So Olio, reluctantly, got up and went to get his order; he turned around and walked back to the table where he'd been sitting, pulled out his game board, and started challenging himself.

Just then, a round, fat man walked into the bar; he must have weighed 100—no, 200 kilos—but in the eyes of those watching him, no one would even dream of mocking him.

The bartender went from complaining about “the youth of today” to complete silence; the only words that came out of his mouth were: “Good evening, Don Polpo!”

Meanwhile, Olio watched the scene in bewilderment.

“Hey, I saw you being rude to this kid!” Polpo said. “What's your name, kid?”

“My name is Olio, sir.”

“And whose son are you?” Polpo asked him.

Before he even had time to say his parents' names, he was interrupted.

“Ah, but you're not from around here! You know you shouldn't be hanging around places like this! There's a war going on here!”

Polpo pointed to the Risk board and then changed his tone: “Olio… Are you playing dumb?”

“Olio, do you want to make some money?”

Olio looked at the big, angry man: “I don't want to sell drugs, sir…”

That's when Polpo burst out laughing: “Drugs? As if a sharp mind like yours would ever sell drugs! Instead… show me how you play.”

The boy, thrilled that someone was interested in his obsession, began to explain: “Right now I'm playing as Red; I'm preparing an attack on Northern Europe from Ukraine. Meanwhile, as Green, seeing the troops massing in Ukraine, I’m defending Southern Europe.”

Polpo thought he must have heard wrong: “But you said you were attacking Northern Europe, not Southern Europe.”

Olio replied, confused: “How am I supposed to know what the other player is planning?”

Polpo thought the kid had a truly remarkable way of thinking: “Look, kid, you don't have to sell drugs for me; I want you to do another job. We're now at war with another organization, and I want you to advise me on the best next move.”

“How am I supposed to know that?”

So Polpo pulled out another Risiko board; this one, instead of the usual world map, depicted Naples divided into neighborhoods. Then he set up the pieces, using yellow out of personal preference: “This is the situation, kid.”

Olio, without giving the morality of the situation a second thought, replied, “The best move is to attack Scampia, sir.”

“Listen, Olio, I have a problem, though. People aren't pieces… they make independent decisions…”

“People don't make independent decisions,” Olio interrupted. “Their choices are determined by what they see, what they hear, what they feel… Scientifically speaking, every consequence stems from an action.”

Polpo was thrilled with this answer: “So—”

“To bring about a consequence, you have to study the action that causes it.”

Regina Coeli, Rome, 1990

“Every action has a consequence; we tried to escape, and they caught us…”

Polnareff glared at Olio angrily: “They haven't caught us yet. Even if he attacks, our Stand users are trained in combat, right, Jotaro?”

“Yes,” Jotaro replied coldly, “but be careful, Polnareff—maybe he's bluffing.”

“I'm not bluffing,” his authoritative voice echoed through the prison cells. “In fact, I'll show you right now.”

He didn't even have time to summon his Stand before Jotaro had already launched his attack.

STAR PLATINUM

Jotaro's massive purple giant's fist passed right through the police chief's Stand, as if it were air.

“Fools! My Stand, Message in a Bottle, can't be attacked! And soon you'll be forced to hand over the prisoner to me.”

“Hey, Jotaro! Don't you notice that the floor is starting to shake?” Polnareff grew concerned.

“You're right! And what's that sound? I've only ever heard a tremor like this from those wooden plank floors when hundreds of people are walking on them—here the floors are stone. What does his Stand power have to do with it?”

“Jotaro! Behind you!”

A horde of thousands of men was rushing to attack the trio; the moment the first guy lunged at Jotaro, time froze:

STAR PĮATINUM: UNLEASH THE WORLD

In the dimension where the clock's hands doesn't tick, Jotaro took a moment to think: “I have 2 seconds left.”

He carefully assessed the situation and noticed the attackers; they looked like perfectly ordinary people: “Damn! These are ordinary people; I can't use my Stand to attack ordinary citizens. That cop must have hypnotized them with his Stand. Time is running out, I have one second left.”

So he shifted his shoulder slightly, and when time resumed, he dodged a guy who was jumping at him: “Polnareff, they're hypnotized. Don't attack them, they're ordinary people. We have to focus on the carrier!”

Polnareff then sprinted toward the police chief, but another equally large group of men blocked his path.

“Jotaro, I'm about to attack—I don't care if they're civilians; it's for the greater good!”

“Stop! As long as the situation stays as it is, I can attack from a distance with Star Finger.”

Olio, on the other hand, just kept smiling: “Just think about where we've ended up—in a real battlefield.”

The crowd was growing; it was so dense and concentrated that it looked like a fluid, yet thick enough to immobilize Jotaro and Polnareff.

They were being crushed so hard that Polnareff could no longer breathe: “What do we do, Jotaro?”

“We're screwed, Polnareff!”
